B-ForeSea

The all-in-one personalized forecast app that helps watersport aficionados and beachgoers enter the ocean with safety and fun.

Type
End-to-end mobile app
Disciplines
UX / UI, Branding
Timeline
~7 months, alongside a full-time job
Process
Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test

The problem

Existing watersport apps overwhelm users with data. B-ForeSea offers beautiful graphics and straightforward wind and wave reports, with a dashboard users can customize to their own preferences.

Empathize

Competitive analysis

I analyzed weather and watersport apps including MagicSeaWeed and Buoycast.

  • Specialist apps are accurate but not all-in-one, so users switch between apps for different activities
  • Most include intrusive ads
  • Data depends on third-party sources (NOAA, NDBC)
  • Complex data needs specialist knowledge to interpret

User research

A survey of 22 respondents and four interviews with Hawaii-based participants aged 35-45 (three watersport enthusiasts, one casual beachgoer).

  • 73% of respondents were male, aged 36-45
  • 80% use forecasts to plan trips
  • Most-wanted features: location search, all-day conditions, 5-day forecasts, activity-based filtering, live beach cameras
  • Core needs: fast access to relevant data, accuracy, a simple interface, beach facilities and nearby businesses

Define

Problem statement: users need a quick, easy way to get clear beach and ocean conditions based on their interests or criteria. Existing apps are overwhelming because of excessive, often irrelevant data.

Hypothesis: a customizable single app with dashboard control, activity-based filtering and local business integration can remove information overload and generate revenue to replace ads.

I built two personas, Joy and Mark, covering different levels of watersport engagement.

Ideate

Three task flows came from the personas. For information architecture, 10 participants sorted 30 cards at a 63% agreement threshold. The initial site map evolved into a final one based on their feedback.

Prototype

The prototype went through three fidelities: hand-sketched paper, an interactive mid-fidelity version for testing, then a refined high-fidelity version. Early dashboards were cluttered, so I introduced a separate home screen with a pull-up dashboard.

Test

Six users (five remote, one in person) took part in 30-40 minute desktop sessions. Tasks: onboarding, customizing the dashboard, finding detailed ocean forecasts, finding calm-water beaches as a visitor, viewing a map location, and finding beach facilities.

What testing showed

  • Edit and personalization controls were unclear
  • The palm tree and edit-dashboard icons confused people
  • The multi-purpose search was unclear
  • Onboarding language created friction

What I changed

  • Simplified and clarified icons
  • Replaced pull-up icons with a 'View All Forecasts' text link
  • Converted search from a dropdown to a segmented menu
  • Changed 'I got it' to 'Skip' in onboarding
  • Added a cleaner home screen

Visual design

Preference testing (65%) favored a blue primary color with yellow accents for a cheerful, lively, fun feel, paired with a simple line-and-filled illustration style.

Key takeaways

  1. Designing for varied user groups means balancing activity-specific preferences with common goals
  2. Simplicity and less complexity have a large effect on the experience
  3. Iterate at every stage
  4. Usability testing surfaces navigation problems even when the functionality is valued

Next: targeted interviews by segment, warnings and favorites, animation, richer beach overviews, and safety alerts with liability disclaimers.
